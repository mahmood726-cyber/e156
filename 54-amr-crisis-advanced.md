# The Antimicrobial Resistance Crisis in Africa: A Trial Registry Deficit Analysis

**Gloria Margaret Nanono et al.**

*Synthēsis Journal of Evidence Synthesis*

---

## Abstract

Africa bears an estimated 1.05 million deaths annually attributable to bacterial antimicrobial resistance, yet only 45 of 24,771 registered African clinical trials (0.2%) in the Aggregate Analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov (AACT) April 2026 snapshot focus on antimicrobial resistance. This structural deficit — the near-total absence of AMR-focused trial activity in the world's most heavily burdened region — reflects four compounding failures: an evidence gap in how newly approved antibiotics perform in African populations, sustained industry disinvestment from antibiotic development, a diagnostic infrastructure deficit that renders AMR deaths invisible, and a research ecosystem in which even the partial exception (MDR-TB) is dominated by sites and investigators based in a handful of southern African centres. Without deliberate structural intervention — mandatory Africa-inclusive trial provisions in WHO AMR action planning, dedicated funding streams, and a pan-African AMR trial network — the 2050 projection of 10 million annual global AMR deaths will land disproportionately on African populations for whom no evidence-based treatment pathways were ever developed.

---

## 1. Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance has been characterised as a "silent pandemic": unlike an acute outbreak, it accumulates invisibly across health systems, one treatment failure at a time, in patients whose cultures are never taken and whose resistance profiles are never mapped. The scale of this silent accumulation is now quantified. Murray and colleagues, in the landmark 2022 Global Burden of Bacterial Antimicrobial Resistance study, estimated 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to bacterial AMR globally in 2019, with a further 4.95 million deaths associated with AMR [1]. Within this global burden, sub-Saharan Africa carried the highest regional all-age mortality rate attributable to AMR, with approximately 1.05 million deaths per year across the African continent — a figure that towers above all other world regions and that represents not a projection but a measured present-day reality [1]. Looking forward, the O'Neill Commission's landmark 2016 review estimated that drug-resistant infections will kill 10 million people annually by 2050 without coordinated global action, surpassing cancer as a cause of death globally [O'Neill 2016 AMR Review Commission]. Africa, given current burden trajectories, will account for a disproportionate share of that projected toll.

The pathogens driving this crisis are well characterised. The ESKAPE group — *Enterococcus faecium*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Acinetobacter baumannii*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, and *Enterobacter* spp. — represents the most dangerous cluster of drug-resistant nosocomial pathogens globally [1]. In African healthcare facilities, gram-negative ESKAPE members — particularly *Klebsiella pneumoniae* and *Acinetobacter baumannii* — are especially prevalent and increasingly resistant to last-resort carbapenem agents. The New Delhi Metallo-β-lactamase (NDM) resistance gene, originally identified in South Asian isolates, has now been documented across multiple African countries, conferring resistance to nearly all β-lactam antibiotics. Simultaneously, mycobacterial resistance drives a parallel crisis: Africa bears approximately 27% of the global tuberculosis burden, with an estimated 78,000 MDR-TB cases arising on the continent annually [WHO Global Tuberculosis Report].

The WHO Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance, adopted in 2015, committed member states to five strategic objectives including surveillance, stewardship, and research into new antimicrobials [2]. Against this backdrop, the near-total absence of AMR-focused clinical trials registered at African sites represents a profound structural failure. The AACT April 2026 snapshot documents 24,771 trials with African site involvement. Of these, only 45 — 0.2% — focus on antimicrobial resistance. This paper characterises that deficit, diagnoses its structural causes, and advocates for a mandatory reorientation of global AMR research investment toward Africa.

---

## 2. Methods

We queried the Aggregate Analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov (AACT) database using the April 12, 2026 snapshot. AMR-focused trials were identified through a multi-term keyword search applied to study titles, brief summaries, detailed descriptions, condition terms, and intervention fields. Search terms included: "antimicrobial resistance," "antibiotic resistance," "drug-resistant," "multidrug-resistant," "MDR," "extensively drug-resistant," "XDR," "carbapenem-resistant," "ESKAPE," "MRSA," "stewardship," "antimicrobial stewardship," "cefiderocol," "ceftazidime-avibactam," "imipenem-relebactam," "meropenem-vaborbactam," "bedaquiline," "pretomanid," and "linezolid" when combined with infectious disease context. Trials were filtered to include only those with at least one registered site in an African country, as defined by the United Nations geoscheme. The primary denominator was all trials with African site involvement in the same snapshot (N = 24,771). We characterised included trials by therapeutic focus area, geographic distribution of sites, and sponsor type. Absence of specific agent trials (cefiderocol, new carbapenem combinations) was confirmed by targeted AACT queries.

## 3. Results

Of 24,771 clinical trials with registered African site involvement in the AACT April 2026 snapshot, 45 (0.2%) addressed antimicrobial resistance as their primary focus. This proportion is dramatically below the African contribution to global AMR mortality, which exceeds 20% of all attributable deaths [1].

The 45 AMR trials decompose across therapeutic focus areas as follows. MDR-TB trials represent the largest identifiable subset, reflecting the sustained research ecosystem around tuberculosis in southern and eastern Africa. A smaller number of trials address ESKAPE gram-negative pathogens — primarily hospital-acquired infections with *Klebsiella pneumoniae* and *Acinetobacter baumannii* — and a further set addresses antimicrobial stewardship interventions in health system contexts. Community-acquired infection trials and trials specifically designed around carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales are minimally represented.

Geographically, the distribution is heavily concentrated. South Africa hosts the largest number of AMR trial sites, consistent with its academic hospital network (including institutions affiliated with the University of the Witwatersrand, the University of Cape Town, and the South African Medical Research Council), its longstanding TB research infrastructure, and its relative concentration of clinical research regulatory capacity. Egypt, which accounts for 15,902 of all 24,771 African AACT trials (64.2%), contributes a share of AMR trials substantially lower than its proportion of total African trial activity, reflecting the predominantly non-infectious-disease focus of Egyptian clinical trial registrations. South Africa accounts for 3,802 trials overall (15.3% of the African total).

By sponsor category, the broader African trial landscape is dominated by non-industry funders: 80.0% are categorised as other or academic sponsors, 13.9% as industry, and only 1.3% as NIH. AMR trials are even more reliant on academic and public funders, with pharmaceutical industry sponsorship largely absent from Africa-based AMR research.

Critically absent from the registry are trials of novel antibiotic agents that have recently received regulatory approval in high-income country settings. Cefiderocol, ceftazidime-avibactam, and imipenem-cilastatin-relebactam — agents approved between 2017 and 2023 for carbapenem-resistant gram-negative infections — have no registered trial activity at African sites in the AACT database. The approximately 15 new antibiotic agents registered globally between 2017 and 2023, the majority of which are derivatives of existing classes rather than mechanistically novel entities, were evaluated almost exclusively in trials conducted in high-income country settings.

The broader context of African trial growth makes this AMR deficit more striking, not less. Total African clinical trial registrations have grown from approximately 35 per year in 2000 to 2,628 per year in 2023, a 75-fold increase reflecting the substantial expansion of clinical research infrastructure across the continent. AMR research has not participated in this growth.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 The Evidence Gap: New Antibiotics Approved Without African Data

The approval of cefiderocol by the United States FDA in 2019 and the European Medicines Agency in 2020 was based on pivotal trials conducted predominantly at sites in North America, Europe, Japan, South Korea, and Australia. The pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic parameters underpinning dosing recommendations were derived from populations in which HIV co-infection rates were negligible, nutritional deficiencies were uncommon, concomitant use of antimalarial and antiretroviral agents was rare, and renal function patterns reflected high-income country comorbidity profiles.

None of these assumptions holds for a substantial proportion of African patients requiring treatment for carbapenem-resistant gram-negative infections. HIV co-infection alters drug distribution volumes, hepatic enzyme induction profiles, and renal elimination pathways for multiple antibiotic classes. Malnutrition — including protein-energy malnutrition affecting albumin-dependent drug binding — is prevalent in high-burden African settings and can substantially alter the free drug fraction of highly protein-bound agents. Concomitant antimalarial therapy, antiretroviral regimens including efavirenz (a potent CYP3A4 inducer), and rifamycin-containing TB regimens all create drug interaction landscapes not studied in pivotal HIC trials [1].

The consequence is that African clinicians treating carbapenem-resistant infections with newly approved agents are operating outside the evidence base for every drug they use. Dosing recommendations may be systematically wrong for the populations facing the highest burden. This is not a theoretical concern — analogous pharmacokinetic differences have been documented for older antibiotic classes in sub-Saharan African populations, including altered aminoglycoside clearance and modified β-lactam pharmacodynamic target attainment in malnourished children [9].

### 4.2 Industry Abandonment and the Africa Penalty

The antibiotic development pipeline has been in structural crisis for decades. The O'Neill Review documented that pharmaceutical companies have exited antibiotic R&D at scale because the commercial model is fundamentally unfavourable: antibiotics are prescribed for short courses, stewardship efforts deliberately limit their use, and resistance erodes market value rapidly [O'Neill 2016]. The approximately 15 new agents registered globally between 2017 and 2023 represent largely derivative chemistry — new combinations and formulations of existing β-lactam and β-lactamase inhibitor pairs — rather than novel mechanism-of-action drugs.

Within this already depleted pipeline, Africa receives a compounding penalty. The small number of industry-sponsored AMR trials that do occur are conducted at sites where regulatory pathways are most efficient, investigator experience is highest, and the commercial market for the approved product is largest. African regulatory authorities, while increasingly capable, represent neither the primary approval pathway nor the primary commercial market for premium-priced novel antibiotics. The 13.9% industry share of overall African clinical trial sponsorship falls further in AMR research. The result is that African patients with drug-resistant infections have no new treatment options being evaluated in their context, while facing resistance rates to existing agents that in some settings exceed those documented in any HIC [4].

This is not merely an equity concern, though it is that. It is also a scientific failure. Drug-resistant pathogens in African settings are subject to different selection pressures, different resistance gene transmission networks, and different co-resistance linkages than those in HIC settings. Carbapenemase-producing organisms at African sites carry resistance gene arrays that may differ structurally from those in European outbreak strains. Clinical trials conducted only in HICs generate evidence for a biological landscape that differs systematically from the one in which the greatest burden falls.

### 4.3 Diagnostic Invisibility and the AMR Surveillance Deficit

A profound but underappreciated driver of the trial deficit is diagnostic invisibility. Murray and colleagues estimated the African AMR burden using indirect methods precisely because direct microbiological surveillance is unavailable at population scale across most of the continent [1]. The GBD 2019 study that provides the denominators for burden estimation similarly relies heavily on modelled rather than directly observed data for sub-Saharan Africa [8].

The mechanism is straightforward. Without functioning microbiology laboratories with culture capacity and antimicrobial susceptibility testing, a patient who dies from a carbapenem-resistant *Klebsiella* infection is recorded as dying from sepsis of unknown aetiology, or pneumonia, or post-operative infection. The resistance is undetected, unfiled, and uncounted. This invisibility has a direct policy consequence: funders, health ministries, and research prioritisation bodies cannot respond to data that does not exist. When AMR deaths are recorded as generic sepsis deaths, the urgency — and the funding — goes to sepsis management rather than to AMR-specific trial development [10].

This diagnostic deficit also undermines stewardship. Effective antimicrobial stewardship requires knowing which organisms are circulating, which resistance patterns are present, and which drugs remain effective locally. Where stewardship programmes exist at all in African LMICs — and they are absent or nascent in most — they operate without the microbiological data infrastructure that makes stewardship evidence-based [4]. The absence of stewardship data further reduces the trial evidence base, since stewardship intervention trials require baseline resistance surveillance data to design endpoints and measure effect.

### 4.4 MDR-TB as Partial Exception — and What It Teaches

The MDR-TB trial ecosystem in Africa represents the clearest demonstration that African AMR trial capacity is achievable when investment is sustained and politically prioritised. South Africa's TB research infrastructure — centred at the University of the Witwatersrand's Clinical HIV Research Unit, the University of Cape Town Lung Institute, the South African MRC, and the THINK TB and HIV Investigative Network — has made the country a primary site for pivotal MDR-TB trials including the TB-PRACTECAL and ZeNix trials evaluating bedaquiline-pretomanid-linezolid regimens (BPaLM) [5].

Yet even this partial exception reveals the limits of the current model. South Africa alone hosts the substantial majority of Africa's MDR-TB trial sites; countries with equal or greater MDR-TB burdens — Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Ethiopia, Kenya — are far less represented as trial sites. African principal investigators, while increasingly present, remain in the minority in pivotal trial leadership roles. And the BPaLM regimen, while transformative, was designed around endpoints and patient populations in which African comorbidity patterns were incorporated only partially. The TB trial ecosystem has also benefited from a unique funding architecture — the NIH-funded AIDS Clinical Trials Group and its HIV-TB co-infection research priorities, combined with Wellcome Trust and EDCTP investment specifically targeting African TB research — that has no equivalent in the broader AMR (non-TB) space.

The lesson is precisely this: focused infrastructure investment, combined with dedicated funding mechanisms and explicit mandates for African site inclusion, can build trial capacity in an AMR disease area. The question is whether the political and funding will exists to replicate this model beyond TB.

---

## 5. Conclusion

The 0.2% AMR trial share documented in this analysis is not a market failure or a logistical oversight. It is the cumulative result of four structural decisions: to approve new antibiotics without requiring evidence in African populations; to permit industry to define research geographies by commercial rather than burden criteria; to allow diagnostic infrastructure deficits to persist unaddressed; and to treat the MDR-TB exception as sufficient evidence that African AMR research is being done. None of these decisions was explicitly made — they emerged from the aggregate of individual funding, regulatory, and institutional choices that systematically excluded Africa from the AMR evidence base.

The corrective requires equal structural deliberateness. The WHO Global Action Plan on AMR, now a decade old, should be revised to mandate Africa-inclusive trial provisions for all new antibiotic registrations submitted to WHO prequalification or Stringent Regulatory Authority approval. A dedicated AMR Clinical Trials Network — modelled on the ACTG but scoped to bacterial resistance beyond TB — should be capitalised by the G7 AMR commitments and multilateral development banks, with African investigator leadership written into governance structures as a requirement rather than an aspiration [11]. Antimicrobial stewardship programme investment, linked to diagnostic laboratory capacity building, must precede and accompany trial development: without resistance surveillance data, neither stewardship nor trials can function as designed [4].

Africa is not a trial site waiting to be activated. It is the region where AMR is already winning, where the evidence base is most needed, and where its absence is most consequential. The 1.05 million annual deaths estimated by Murray and colleagues [1] are occurring now, in populations for whom no clinical trial has evaluated whether the newest antibiotics work, at what dose, in the presence of the comorbidities they carry. That gap is the AMR crisis — not just the resistance itself, but the deliberate failure to generate the knowledge needed to address it where it is worst.
