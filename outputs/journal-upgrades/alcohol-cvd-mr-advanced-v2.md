## Abstract

**Background.** Observational cohorts report a J-shaped alcohol–coronary-heart-disease
(CHD) association (light drinkers ~29% lower risk; Ronksley 2011, RR 0.71). Whether this
is causal or an artifact of "sick-quitter" abstainer heterogeneity is contested.
Mendelian randomisation (MR) uses germline alcohol-metabolism variants (ADH1B rs1229984,
ALDH2 rs671) as instruments immune to reverse causation and conventional confounding.

**Methods.** We triangulated four landmark MR studies (Holmes 2014, Millwood 2019,
Larsson 2020, Biddinger 2022; combined N > 1.4 million across European, East-Asian, and
UK-Biobank populations), harmonising all estimates to the effect of *higher* genetically-
predicted alcohol, and contrasted them with the observational benchmark. Instruments and
outcomes were too heterogeneous to pool; we report structured triangulation.

**Results.** Every MR study points **null-to-harmful**. Holmes: carriers of the
alcohol-*lowering* ADH1B A-allele had **lower** CHD (OR 0.90, 0.84–0.96) and lower
ischaemic stroke (0.83, 0.72–0.95) — i.e. per higher alcohol, OR ≈ **1.11** — with a more
favourable blood-pressure, IL-6, and adiposity profile. Larsson: coronary artery disease
(CAD) OR **1.16** (1.00–1.36) and stroke **1.27** (1.12–1.45) per higher alcohol.
Biddinger: CAD OR **1.4** (1.1–1.8), nonlinear (heavy intake exponentially harmful).
Millwood (ALDH2 natural experiment, China): genotype-predicted alcohol raised blood
pressure and stroke (ischaemic 1.27; intracerebral haemorrhage 1.58) with little net
effect on myocardial infarction (MI 0.96, 0.78–1.18, NS). The observational "protection"
(0.71) overstates the causal CHD effect by **56–97%**.

**Conclusion.** MR does not reproduce the J-curve. The observational cardioprotection of
light drinking is largely confounding, not causation. There is no genetically supported
cardioprotective dose; guidance describing light drinking as "heart-healthy" is not
supported by causal evidence.

## 1. Introduction

For three decades, observational epidemiology has reported that light-to-moderate drinkers
have lower coronary risk than abstainers — a J- or U-shaped curve (Ronksley 2011: incident
CHD RR 0.71). Two rival explanations exist. The *causal* account invokes plausible biology
(HDL elevation, antithrombotic effects). The *confounding* account invokes the
"sick-quitter" phenomenon (Shaper 1988): former drinkers who quit because of ill health
are misclassified into the abstainer reference group, dragging its risk upward and
manufacturing apparent benefit for continued light drinkers; residual confounding by
socioeconomic and lifestyle factors compounds this. Randomised trials of alcohol for
cardiovascular prevention are ethically and practically infeasible, so causation has been
untestable by the usual gold standard — until Mendelian randomisation. Because alcohol-
metabolism genotypes are assigned at conception, independent of lifestyle, and affect
lifetime exposure, they provide an instrument for the causal effect of alcohol free of
reverse causation and conventional confounding. This paper triangulates the four landmark
MR studies to adjudicate the J-curve.

## 2. Methods

We identified MR studies using ADH1B rs1229984 and/or ALDH2 rs671 as instruments for
alcohol intake with cardiovascular outcomes, applying inverse-variance-weighted (IVW)
primary analyses with weighted-median/MR-Egger sensitivity. Four met full eligibility
(Holmes 2014, Millwood 2019, Larsson 2020, Biddinger 2022). Because the studies use
different instruments (ADH1B vs ALDH2), populations (European, East Asian, UK Biobank),
and outcome definitions, formal pooling is inappropriate; we report structured
triangulation. Critically, all estimates were **harmonised to a single direction — the
effect of higher genetically-predicted alcohol on CVD** — because Holmes reports its
result per alcohol-*lowering* allele, which is frequently misread. We contrasted the MR
estimates with the observational benchmark (Ronksley RR 0.71) and computed the
confounding gap (MR ÷ observational). All values are emitted by
`alcohol-cvd-mr-verify.py`.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Triangulation table (all directions = higher alcohol → CVD)

| Source | Design / population | CHD/CAD | Stroke |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ronksley 2011 (observational) | meta of cohorts | **RR 0.71** (protective) | ~0.75 (protective) |
| Holmes 2014 (MR, ADH1B) | European, N=261,991 | **OR ≈1.11** (from 0.90 per alcohol-lowering allele) | ischaemic ≈1.20 (harmful) |
| Larsson 2020 (MR, IVW) | European | **OR 1.16** (1.00–1.36) | **1.27** (1.12–1.45) |
| Biddinger 2022 (MR) | UK Biobank, N=371,463 | **OR 1.40** (1.1–1.8) | — |
| Millwood 2019 (MR, ALDH2) | East Asian, N≈512,715 | MI 0.96 (0.78–1.18, NS) | ischaemic 1.27; ICH 1.58 |

### 3.2 The Holmes harmonisation — the crux

Holmes is the most-cited and most-misread study. The ADH1B A-allele *lowers* alcohol
intake (−17.2% units/week per allele, higher abstention) and carriers had **lower** CHD
(OR 0.90, 0.84–0.96), lower ischaemic stroke (0.83), lower systolic blood pressure
(−0.88 mmHg), lower IL-6, waist, and BMI. Read correctly, this says: **less alcohol →
better cardiovascular profile and less coronary disease**. Re-expressed per *higher*
alcohol, the CHD OR is ≈1.11 — harmful, the opposite of the J-curve. The protective
association held across all baseline drinking categories (p=0.83 for heterogeneity),
directly contradicting a threshold below which alcohol helps.

### 3.3 The confounding gap

| Comparison (CHD) | Observational | MR (causal) | Ratio | Observational overstatement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| vs Holmes-implied | 0.71 | 1.11 | 1.56× | ~56% |
| vs Larsson | 0.71 | 1.16 | 1.63× | ~63% |
| vs Biddinger | 0.71 | 1.40 | 1.97× | ~97% |

The observational "protection" (0.71) and the causal MR estimate (≈1.0–1.4) differ by a
factor of 1.6–2.0. This gap *is* the confounding — the manufactured benefit produced by
abstainer heterogeneity and residual lifestyle confounding.

### 3.4 The ALDH2 natural experiment (Millwood)

The strongest test comes from East Asia. The ALDH2 rs671 variant causes an aversive
flushing reaction and dramatically lowers alcohol intake — a near-ideal natural randomiser
— and its effect on drinking is confined to men (few East-Asian women drink), providing a
built-in negative control. In the 512,000-participant China Kadoorie study, genotype-
predicted alcohol raised systolic blood pressure and stroke in men (both p<0.0001) but
not in women (who do not drink despite carrying the genotype) — exactly the pattern
expected if the effect is *causal via alcohol* and not pleiotropic. The authors'
conclusion is unambiguous: "the apparently protective effects of moderate alcohol intake
against stroke are largely non-causal."

### 3.5 Consistency and the MI exception

Four MR studies, three continents, two independent instrument systems (ADH1B and ALDH2),
and multiple outcome definitions converge: no cardioprotection, clear harm for stroke and
blood pressure. The one genuine uncertainty is **myocardial infarction**: Millwood found a
null MR effect (0.96, 0.78–1.18), so alcohol's effect on MI specifically may be small —
but "little net effect on MI" is a long way from the observational "29% protection," and
it does not extend to stroke, where the harm is unequivocal.

## 4. Discussion

Mendelian randomisation delivers a clear verdict on a question observational epidemiology
could not settle: the J-shaped alcohol–CHD curve is not causal. When alcohol exposure is
instrumented by genotype — removing reverse causation and lifestyle confounding — the
apparent protection disappears and the direction reverses toward harm, most decisively for
stroke and blood pressure. The convergence is remarkable precisely because the four studies
share almost nothing methodologically: different genes, different ancestries, different
outcomes, different analysts. That such heterogeneous designs all fail to reproduce the
J-curve, and all point null-to-harmful, is far stronger evidence than any single large
cohort.

The public-health implication is direct. The "light drinking is heart-healthy" message —
still embedded in some guidelines and widely held by the public — rests on observational
associations that MR shows to be confounded by a factor of roughly two. There is no
genetically supported cardioprotective dose of alcohol; the blood-pressure and stroke harm
begins at low intake and rises steeply (Biddinger's nonlinear analysis: light intake
minimal excess risk, heavy intake exponential). Whatever residual uncertainty remains about
MI specifically, it cannot rehabilitate a general cardioprotection claim in the face of
unequivocal stroke and hypertension harm.

The methodological lesson is equally important: this is a textbook case of **triangulation**
resolving a confounded observational signal, and of the necessity of harmonising effect
directions. The Holmes study has been cited on both sides of this debate purely because its
"per alcohol-lowering allele" framing is read carelessly; expressed per higher alcohol, it
sits squarely with the harmful consensus. Effect-direction discipline is not pedantry — here
it is the difference between concluding alcohol protects and concluding it harms.

## 5. Limitations

MR assumes the instrument affects the outcome only through alcohol (no horizontal
pleiotropy); ADH1B and ALDH2 are strong, specific instruments, but pleiotropy cannot be
fully excluded (weighted-median and MR-Egger sensitivities in the source studies mitigate
this). Instrument–population specificity (ADH1B in Europeans, ALDH2 in East Asians) limits
transportability, though the cross-population convergence is a strength. The MI effect is
genuinely uncertain (Millwood null). The specific per-280 g/week Millwood stroke/MI RRs and
Larsson's stroke OR are from the full texts (the abstracts confirm direction and
significance) and are flagged accordingly. No formal pooling was performed, by design.

## 6. Conclusion

Across four independent Mendelian-randomisation studies spanning >1.4 million participants
and two instrument systems, higher genetically-predicted alcohol is null-to-harmful for
coronary disease (OR ≈1.1–1.4) and clearly harmful for stroke and blood pressure — none
reproduce the observational J-curve, which overstates protection by 56–97%. The
cardioprotection of light drinking is confounding, not causation. There is no genetically
supported "heart-healthy" dose of alcohol.
