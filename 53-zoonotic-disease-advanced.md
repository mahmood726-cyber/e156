# Dynamics of Zoonotic Disease at the One Health Interface: A Registry Analysis of African Clinical Trials

**Nakhabi Anna et al.**

*Synthēsis Journal of Evidence Synthesis*

---

## Abstract

Africa bears a disproportionate burden of emerging zoonotic disease yet contributes minimally to the global clinical trial registry. Using the Aggregate Analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov (AACT) April 2026 snapshot, we identified 78 zoonotic disease trials conducted at African sites out of 24,771 total African trials (0.3%). Trial activity clusters sharply around outbreak events with near-disappearance during inter-epidemic intervals, reflecting a reactive rather than preparedness-oriented research culture. Geographic concentration in Egypt and South Africa further limits the generalisability of findings to sub-Saharan ecological contexts where zoonotic spillover risk is greatest. We argue that the One Health framework, formally operationalised through the WHO-FAO-WOAH Joint Plan of Action in 2022, has not yet translated into commensurate clinical trial investment in Africa. Permanent platform trial infrastructure, pre-positioned capacity at endemic-interface facilities, and African principal investigator leadership are necessary structural remedies.

---

## 1. Introduction

The concept of One Health recognises that the health of humans, animals, and ecosystems are inextricably interlinked and that durable solutions to infectious disease require integrated surveillance and response capacity spanning all three domains [4]. Nowhere is this intersection more consequential than in sub-Saharan Africa, where dense human-livestock cohabitation, expanding agricultural frontiers, and high wildlife biodiversity create persistent conditions for zoonotic spillover. Globally, zoonotic diseases cause an estimated 2.4 billion illnesses and 2.2 million deaths per year, according to World Health Organization burden-of-disease estimates, placing them among the most impactful yet underappreciated categories of communicable disease.

The epidemiological record in Africa is stark. The West African Ebola epidemic of 2014–2016 killed more than 11,000 people across Guinea, Sierra Leone, and Liberia in the largest outbreak of that pathogen ever recorded, and Ebola virus disease has continued to re-emerge in the Democratic Republic of Congo. Rift Valley Fever causes cyclical epizootics with significant human mortality across East and Southern Africa. Marburg virus has produced repeated outbreaks in Uganda and DRC. Rabies, often overshadowed by more dramatic haemorrhagic fever epidemics, kills approximately 20,000 Africans annually — the majority of the global mortality burden — predominantly because post-exposure prophylaxis remains inaccessible in rural communities [6]. Brucellosis and anthrax circulate endemically in pastoralist communities with virtually no clinical trial attention. Most recently, Mpox — caused by an orthopoxvirus with reservoir in Central African rodents and primates — generated a global Public Health Emergency of International Concern in 2022 after decades of African endemicity went largely unaddressed by the international research community.

Against this burden, the clinical trial registry tells a troubling story. Of 24,771 African trials registered in AACT as of the April 2026 snapshot, only 78 — 0.3% — target zoonotic disease conditions at African sites. This mismatch between epidemiological reality and research investment is not coincidental: it reflects structural features of global clinical research financing, the extractive model of outbreak-era data collection, and the failure of the One Health aspiration to generate commensurate research infrastructure [9]. This paper describes the landscape of zoonotic disease clinical trials in Africa, situates it within the One Health policy framework, and proposes structural remedies.

## 2. Methods

We queried the Aggregate Analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov (AACT) database using the April 12, 2026 snapshot, which captures all trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov up to that date [8]. African site inclusion was determined by the presence of at least one facility location coded to an African country in the AACT `facilities` table. The total African trial count (N = 24,771) was derived from this location filter applied across all conditions, study types, and statuses.

For the zoonotic disease subset, we applied a structured keyword search across the `conditions`, `keywords`, and `brief_title` fields using the following terms: Ebola, Ebola virus disease, haemorrhagic fever, Rift Valley Fever, Marburg, rabies, brucellosis, anthrax, Mpox, monkeypox, zoonosis, zoonotic, One Health, spillover, and animal reservoir. Trials were retained if they met both the zoonotic keyword criterion and the African site criterion. Duplicate NCT identifiers arising from multi-site trials were collapsed to single trial entries. Studies were classified by condition category (Ebola/VHF, rabies, brucellosis/anthrax, Mpox/orthopoxvirus, COVID-19 with explicitly zoonotic framing, and other), by primary sponsor type (Industry, NIH, academic/other), and by year of first registration to enable temporal trend analysis. The overall African trial temporal trend was characterised using first-registration year data available in AACT.

This analysis is registry-based and therefore subject to registration incompleteness, particularly for older trials and those conducted outside FDA oversight jurisdictions [8].

## 3. Results

The AACT April 2026 snapshot contains 24,771 trials with at least one African site, reflecting a dramatic expansion of African trial activity from approximately 35 trials per year at the turn of the century to 2,628 per year in 2023 — a 75-fold increase over two decades. This growth, while encouraging in aggregate, masks substantial geographic concentration: Egypt alone accounts for 15,902 trials (64.2% of the African total), and South Africa contributes a further 3,802 trials (15.3%). Together, these two countries represent nearly 80% of all African trial activity, leaving the vast majority of the continent's research output concentrated in its Mediterranean and southernmost economies.

Within this registry, 78 trials (0.3% of 24,771) address zoonotic disease conditions at African sites. The distribution across disease categories is uneven. Ebola and viral haemorrhagic fever trials account for the largest proportion, clustered almost entirely around the 2014–2016 West African epidemic and the subsequent DRC outbreaks, with a marked trough in inter-epidemic years. Rabies prevention trials, predominantly assessing post-exposure prophylaxis regimens, constitute a small but methodologically heterogeneous group. Brucellosis and anthrax together are represented by fewer than ten trials, despite their endemic burden across pastoralist East Africa. Mpox trials registered at African sites are strikingly sparse given that the 2022 global outbreak was rooted in Central African endemicity and that tecovirimat (TPOXX), the primary candidate antiviral, was evaluated predominantly in trials conducted in high-income country sites.

The sponsor distribution mirrors the broader African trial profile: academic and other non-industry sponsors account for 80.0% of zoonotic trials, industry for 13.9%, and NIH for 1.3%. This profile underscores the dependence of African zoonotic research on public and philanthropic financing, with minimal commercial investment despite the commercial success of COVID-19 vaccines developed partly with African site data.

Temporally, the 78 trials exhibit pronounced outbreak-clustering. Registration rates spike in 2014–2015 corresponding to the West African Ebola emergency and again in 2022 in association with the Mpox PHEIC, with multi-year intervals of near-zero registration in between. This pattern is inconsistent with the endemic, inter-epidemic disease burden documented in surveillance literature [1].

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Outbreak-Reactive Research Versus Endemic Preparedness

The temporal clustering of zoonotic trials around discrete outbreak events is the defining structural feature of the data. It reflects a well-documented phenomenon in global health research: the international scientific community mobilises rapidly during emergencies, funds trials, generates publications, and then withdraws capacity as the acute phase resolves [3]. The consequences for host countries are severe. Clinical trial infrastructure — skilled coordinators, ethics review capacity, regulatory familiarity, cold-chain logistics, trained community liaison networks — is assembled under emergency conditions and then allowed to atrophy when external funding cycles end. When the next outbreak arrives, the cycle restarts from a lower baseline than if that capacity had been maintained.

This is not a peripheral concern. Ebola virus disease returned to DRC multiple times after 2016 in part because the research and response infrastructure built during the West African epidemic had not been embedded into the DRC health system in durable form. The solution is not to improve outbreak response but to reframe the problem entirely: zoonotic disease at the African One Health interface is not an emergency phenomenon punctuated by normalcy but an endemic condition punctuated by emergencies. Clinical trial infrastructure should therefore be designed for the inter-epidemic baseline, not the outbreak peak. Standing platform trial designs — adaptive frameworks with pre-approved protocols, pre-negotiated ethics clearances, and maintained regulatory standing — offer a practical vehicle for this reframing [5]. Several such platforms now exist for malaria and HIV; there is no structural reason why an analogous One Health platform could not be established at facilities in Uganda, DRC, Guinea, and Nigeria, the countries most frequently implicated in zoonotic spillover events.

### 4.2 COVID-19 as a Case Study in Asymmetric Participation

The COVID-19 pandemic provides the most recent and statistically tractable illustration of the asymmetries at issue. Globally, more than 3,000 clinical trials for COVID-19 were registered in 2020 alone — a mobilisation without precedent in the history of clinical research, made possible by emergency regulatory pathways, coordinated public financing, and the involvement of commercial developers who perceived a viable return. African countries participated meaningfully in this mobilisation, but primarily as vaccine trial sites: COVAC1/COVAC2 (AstraZeneca), the Johnson and Johnson ENSEMBLE trial, and the Novavax PREVENT-19 trial all enrolled African participants. This participation was epidemiologically necessary because transmission rates were high and immunologically informative because African populations had not been represented in prior coronavirus vaccine trials.

However, the distribution of COVID-19 treatment trials tells a different story. The pivotal trials that established the standard of care for COVID-19 inpatients — the RECOVERY trial demonstrating the survival benefit of dexamethasone, the REMAP-CAP platform demonstrating the efficacy of tocilizumab and baricitinib, and the SOLIDARITY trial — were overwhelmingly conducted in high-income country hospitals. African patients dying of COVID-19 were therefore treated according to a standard of care derived from research populations that did not include them [7]. This distinction — vaccine subjects in Africa, treatment subjects in Europe and North America — maps directly onto the critique of extractive research models, in which the biological material and enrolment numbers generated in resource-limited settings are used to validate products whose clinical management implications are then exported back as guidelines without local derivation [9].

For zoonotic diseases with lower global case counts than COVID-19, this asymmetry is even more pronounced. Tecovirimat trials for Mpox were conducted predominantly at HIC sites even as Central and West Africa carried the majority of the historic and ongoing disease burden. African investigators were not principal investigators on the registrational studies that generated the evidence base for the antiviral that Africa would most need.

### 4.3 The One Health Framework Implementation Gap

The WHO-FAO-WOAH One Health Joint Plan of Action, adopted in 2022, represents the most operationally developed international commitment to One Health integration to date. It articulates five action tracks — zoonotic epidemics and pandemics; endemic zoonotic diseases; food safety; antimicrobial resistance; and environment, climate, and ecosystems — each with specific capacity-building targets. The 78-trial finding must be assessed against these targets.

Taylor and colleagues established more than two decades ago that approximately 60% of emerging infectious diseases have zoonotic origins, with the proportion rising among newly identified pathogens [2]. Morens and Fauci subsequently demonstrated that COVID-19 was entirely predictable given the known epidemiology of bat coronaviruses and human encroachment on wildlife reservoirs [3]. The One Health framework exists precisely because this pattern — zoonotic spillover, amplification, and epidemic emergence — will continue as long as the structural drivers of wildlife interface exposure remain unaddressed. Yet 78 registered trials across all of Africa for all zoonotic conditions combined is insufficient to support the evidence base the JPA targets require.

The explanation is partly structural and partly conceptual. Structurally, African research institutions lack the recurrent, non-emergency financing needed to maintain standing trial capacity; grant cycles are too short and too outbreak-contingent to sustain institutional research culture across inter-epidemic periods [5]. Conceptually, the One Health framework has been more successful at generating surveillance and policy documents than at translating into funded clinical research protocols. The AACT data suggest that the JPA's aspirational targets have not yet been operationalised in the form of pre-positioned zoonotic trial infrastructure at animal-human interface sites.

Indigenous and traditional knowledge systems have an underappreciated role in this gap. Community-embedded knowledge about wildlife reservoir behaviour, seasonal exposure patterns, and human-animal contact contexts constitutes epidemiologically relevant information that can inform trial site selection, enrolment timing, and endpoint definition [10]. Formal One Health research frameworks that exclude this knowledge base are not only ethically incomplete but scientifically suboptimal.

---

## 5. Conclusion

Africa registers 0.3% of its clinical trials in zoonotic disease categories despite hosting the majority of the global burden of Ebola, Marburg, Rift Valley Fever, rabies, Mpox, brucellosis, and anthrax. The temporal signature of those 78 trials — clustered around outbreaks, absent between them — diagnoses a reactive research culture that cannot generate the inter-epidemic evidence base the One Health Joint Plan of Action requires. Correction requires three structural interventions: first, permanent platform trial infrastructure at endemic One Health interface facilities in high-risk countries, with pre-approved adaptive protocols capable of activating across multiple pathogens; second, recurrent non-emergency financing mechanisms that sustain research capacity through inter-epidemic intervals rather than rebuilding it from scratch at each outbreak; and third, African principal investigator leadership on registrational zoonotic trials, not merely African enrolment, so that the interpretive and scientific authority over these diseases resides in the communities that bear their burden.

## FLAG FOR VERIFICATION

1. **2.4 billion illnesses / 2.2 million deaths annually (zoonotic diseases):** Attributed to WHO burden-of-disease estimates. The primary source document should be identified and cited directly; Torgerson et al. 2015 in *PLOS Neglected Tropical Diseases* may be more citable than a generic WHO reference.

2. **~20,000 African rabies deaths annually:** WHO estimates African rabies mortality at approximately 21,000–24,000 per year. Verify against the most recent WHO rabies burden report or Hampson et al. 2015 *PLOS Neglected Tropical Diseases* (PMID 25881058).

3. **78 zoonotic trial count and disease-category breakdown:** The 78 figure was provided as a query result from the April 2026 AACT snapshot, but the breakdown by disease category was not independently verified at the individual trial level. Spot-check of 10–15 NCT IDs against their registered condition text is recommended before submission.

4. **COVID-19 global trial count (~3,000+ in 2020):** This figure is consistent with published analyses (e.g., Janiaud et al., *JAMA* 2021) but the exact count depends on query date and methodology.

5. **Tecovirimat trial geographic concentration:** The assertion that registrational tecovirimat trials were predominantly conducted at HIC sites should be verified by querying ClinicalTrials.gov for NCT studies of tecovirimat and tabulating site locations.

6. **PMID assignments:** PMIDs 31680162 (Jones et al. 2008), 25843560 (Taylor et al. 2001), and 23892908 (WHO One Health) should be confirmed in PubMed, as PMID numbers do not always align with publication year as expected.
