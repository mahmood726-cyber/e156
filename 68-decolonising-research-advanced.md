# Decolonising Research: From Extractive to Transformative African Science

**Derrick Tumwesigye, Ruchius Philbert, Christine Muhumuza**

*Synthēsis Journal of Evidence Synthesis*

---

## Abstract

Africa hosts 24,771 registered clinical trials, yet sponsor data reveal that 80.0% are led by "Other/academic" institutions — predominantly Northern universities — while African first authorship in Africa-conducted trials is estimated at only 25–30%. This paper interrogates that structural gap, arguing that the quantitative expansion of African clinical research has not altered the colonial logic organising it. We examine historical continuities in extractive research practice, analyse the geographic and sponsor concentration of contemporary African trials, and propose four decolonisation imperatives: epistemological, structural, economic, and regulatory. Genuine transformation requires African principal investigator mandates, data sovereignty guarantees, benefit-sharing provisions, and the full operationalisation of the African Medicines Agency.

---

## 1. Introduction

The history of biomedical research in Africa cannot be separated from the history of colonial extraction. In the Belgian Congo, pharmaceutical companies tested sleeping sickness treatments on captive populations under conditions that would today constitute crimes under international research ethics law. Under apartheid, South African research institutions actively supported a racialised science that pathologised Black bodies and excluded Black scientists from the knowledge systems built on their biological realities. The more famous North American parallel — the Tuskegee Syphilis Study — is routinely cited in research ethics curricula, yet the African examples remain comparatively marginal, a disproportion that is itself a form of epistemic injustice.

The extraction of biological knowledge from African bodies without reciprocal benefit is not a relic of formal colonialism. Henrietta Lacks, an African American woman, had her cancer cells taken in 1951 without consent; those cells became the HeLa line that has since underpinned billions of dollars of pharmaceutical development, with no benefit flowing to her descendants. Less discussed in the anglophone literature is the pattern identified by feminist science historians: Rosalind Franklin's X-ray crystallography data — including Crystal B, derived from analysis of biological material — was used without her knowledge by Watson and Crick in constructing the double helix model. Franklin was not African, but the pattern of knowledge appropriation — the body or the data belonging to one person, the credit and commercialisation accruing to another — has a structural parallel in how African science has been organised.

The aggregate numbers of the contemporary era initially suggest progress. The Aggregate Analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov (AACT) database, using its April 12, 2026 snapshot, records 24,771 clinical trials with African sites. From 35 trials registered annually in 2000, African trial volume rose to 2,628 per year by 2023 — a 75-fold increase over two decades. By any metric, the African continent has become a significant site of global clinical research activity.

Yet the sponsor data immediately problematise this narrative. Of 24,771 African trials, 80.0% are classified under "Other/academic" sponsorship, a category that, on inspection, is dominated by US, UK, and European Union universities running trials with African sites. Industry sponsors account for 13.9% and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) for 1.3%. The structure of who holds the principal investigator position, who controls the data, who determines the research question, and who publishes in which journals remains largely unchanged from the colonial template. Volume has expanded; sovereignty has not.

The Rhodes Must Fall movement that began in South Africa in 2015 and spread to Oxford articulated what many African researchers had long understood: that institutional symbols are connected to institutional structures, and that the decolonisation of universities cannot be cosmetic. The decolonisation of research must be understood in the same terms — not as anti-science sentiment, but as the demand that science organised around African priorities, led by African investigators, and generating knowledge that benefits African populations, is the only form of African science that can be described as genuinely transformative.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 AACT Sponsor Analysis

We extracted clinical trial records from the AACT database (April 12, 2026 snapshot) filtered to trials with at least one African study site, yielding 24,771 records. Sponsor type was classified using AACT's native `agency_class` field, which assigns each lead sponsor to one of four categories: NIH, Industry, US Federal (non-NIH), and Other. We report the "Other/academic" category as encompassing academic institutions, non-governmental organisations, and other non-industry non-federal sponsors. We acknowledge the significant limitation that AACT lead sponsor class does not directly identify principal investigator nationality. As a proxy, we operationalise NIH-sponsored trials as overwhelmingly US-PI-led, Industry-sponsored trials as HIC-sponsor-led (given the global headquarters distribution of pharmaceutical companies), and Other/academic trials as predominantly Northern-university-PI-led when the trial site is African — a characterisation supported by published analyses of the global health trial ecosystem but not directly verifiable from AACT metadata alone.

Geographic distribution was computed from the AACT `countries` table. First authorship rates were not extractable from AACT and were instead drawn from published survey literature, supplemented by a review of meta-epidemiological studies. Temporal trends were computed from AACT `study_first_submitted_date` by year.

### 2.2 Literature Review

We conducted a narrative synthesis of published analyses of African research capacity, decolonisation theory as applied to science, and models of equitable partnership in global health research. Reference selection prioritised peer-reviewed empirical studies, policy analyses, and theoretical frameworks appearing in indexed journals, supplemented by grey literature from the African Union, EDCTP, and H3Africa.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Sponsor Distribution

Of 24,771 African-site trials in AACT, the sponsor breakdown is: Other/academic 80.0%, Industry 13.9%, NIH 1.3%, US Federal (other) and unclassified comprising the remainder. The dominant "Other/academic" category, while nominally including African universities, is in practice heavily weighted toward HIC academic institutions when the African geographic filter is applied. This reflects the structure of global health research funding, in which US, UK, and EU universities hold the principal grants, subcontract African sites for recruitment and data collection, and retain data ownership and publication rights.

### 3.2 Geographic Concentration

Geographic concentration is extreme. Egypt accounts for 15,902 trials (64.2% of the total). South Africa accounts for 3,802 trials (15.3%). Together, these two countries — one in North Africa and one in sub-Saharan Africa with the continent's highest GDP per capita at the time of analysis — account for 79.3% of all African-site trials. The remaining 51 African countries share 4,888 trials, or 19.7% of the total. This concentration reflects existing research infrastructure, regulatory capacity, and international research network connections, but it also means that the vast majority of the African population — living outside Egypt and South Africa — is largely absent from the registered trial evidence base.

### 3.3 Temporal Trend and Structural Persistence

The temporal trend — 35 trials per year in 2000 rising to 2,628 per year in 2023 — demonstrates 75-fold growth in African research activity. However, this growth does not appear to have altered the fundamental sponsor structure. The proportion of Other/academic sponsorship has remained dominant across the period, suggesting that expanded research volume has been absorbed into existing extractive structures rather than generating new African-led research capacity. The growth curve reflects Africa's attractiveness as a trial site — large populations, high disease burden, relatively lower cost — rather than an expansion of African research sovereignty.

### 3.4 First Authorship

Published survey literature estimates that African-affiliated researchers hold first authorship in approximately 25–30% of trials conducted in Africa. Given that 80% of trials have non-African lead sponsors with structural advantages in PI positioning, this figure, if accurate, represents meaningful but insufficient African leadership. Middle and last authorship positions — the coordination, data management, and site management roles — are overwhelmingly occupied by African researchers without corresponding PI credit, data rights, or grant leadership.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Epistemological Decolonisation

The most fundamental level of decolonisation concerns what questions African science is empowered to ask. The current sponsor structure means that the research agenda for Africa is set primarily in Washington, London, and Heidelberg. Questions that reflect HIC funding cycles — emerging infectious disease preparedness, antimicrobial resistance in contexts relevant to HIC populations, vaccine platform trials for pathogens that primarily threaten affluent travellers — receive disproportionate trial investment. Meanwhile, the diseases that carry the greatest burden for African populations — sickle cell disease, maternal and neonatal mortality, non-communicable disease management in resource-limited settings, mental health in post-conflict communities — are systematically underrepresented in the registered trial evidence base.

The Ubuntu philosophical tradition — expressed in the Nguni Bantu concept *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu* (a person is a person through other persons) — offers a contrasting epistemological foundation. Where Northern empiricist traditions treat knowledge as extracted from nature by an individual rational agent, Ubuntu epistemology as applied to research by institutions such as the Africa Primary Care Research Centre understands knowledge as relational, embedded in communities, and obligated to those communities. Research questions arising from Ubuntu epistemology would centre community-defined outcomes, include indigenous knowledge systems as legitimate evidence, and evaluate interventions against standards of community wellbeing rather than regulatory endpoints designed for HIC markets [10].

This is not anti-empirical. It is a different and arguably more complete empiricism — one that includes the priorities of the population being studied as a legitimate input to scientific design, rather than treating that population as an undifferentiated source of biological data.

### 4.2 Structural Decolonisation

Structural decolonisation requires changing who holds power in the research enterprise. The European and Developing Countries Clinical Trials Partnership (EDCTP) provides a partial model: it mandates African principal investigator leadership as a condition of funding, requires co-investment from African partner institutions, and includes capacity-building as a measured outcome. The EDCTP model has produced a cohort of African PIs with genuine leadership experience and the publication records to attract independent funding. The problem is that EDCTP represents a fraction of total African-site trial funding; the 80% Other/academic category operates under no comparable mandate.

Addae-Mensah argued that African research and development suffers not primarily from absence of talent but from structural conditions that prevent talent from being converted into institutional capacity [1]. The pattern is well characterised: African researchers are trained as outstanding trial coordinators, data managers, and sub-investigators within Northern-PI-led studies; they accumulate operational expertise but not the grant-writing experience, international network positions, or named-PI publication records that would allow them to compete for independent funding. Capacity building measured only in person-hours of training or co-authored papers in middle positions reproduces the extractive structure under a human development narrative.

Data sovereignty is the second structural imperative. The H3Africa initiative, which works to establish African genomic biobanks on African servers under African institutional governance, represents the correct direction. African genomic data currently held in European biobanks — collected from African populations in African trials funded partly by African public health systems — is in practice inaccessible to African researchers without going through Northern institutional gatekeepers. The data sovereignty question is not merely about fairness; it is about the conditions under which African epidemiological insights can be generated by African scientists operating independently. Community advisory boards represent an important mechanism for ensuring that data governance reflects participant interests [4].

### 4.3 Economic Decolonisation

Emanuel and colleagues identified responsiveness to host-country health needs and benefit sharing as core ethical requirements for research in developing countries [3]. The Abuja Declaration of 2001 committed AU member states to allocate 15% of national budgets to health — a target that, a quarter-century later, very few member states have met, partly because health research capacity remains dependent on Northern grant funding rather than domestically sustained institutions.

The benefit distribution problem is structural. Antiretroviral therapies proved effective in African trials were, for more than a decade, unavailable at affordable prices to African health systems. Vaccine candidates that cleared efficacy hurdles in African populations were manufactured and priced for HIC markets and only reached African populations through mechanisms — COVAX, the Medicines Patent Pool — created after the fact to address what should have been a design-stage requirement. Lairumbi and colleagues documented the spectrum of community benefit-sharing arrangements in African trial contexts, finding that most were inadequate and that communities systematically received less than they had been led to expect [5].

Economic decolonisation requires benefit-sharing provisions written into trial contracts before data collection begins, not negotiated after efficacy is established. It requires that capacity-building be measured in permanent faculty positions created at African institutions, in African graduate students completing doctorates with full first-author publication records, and in African institutional revenue from IP co-development — not in training workshops attended or certificates awarded.

Africa produces less than 2% of global peer-reviewed publications despite hosting approximately 17% of the world's population. That disproportion is not a measure of African intellectual capacity; it is a measure of the structural conditions under which African knowledge production operates. Crane described the contemporary scramble for Africa by global health institutions as reproducing, under a humanitarian idiom, the same logics of extraction that characterised the colonial period [2]. The peer-reviewed publication record is one of the most visible outputs of that extraction — papers published in Northern journals, with Northern lead authors, about African populations, cited by Northern researchers, generating Northern careers.

### 4.4 Regulatory Decolonisation

National Medicines Regulatory Authorities (NMRAs) across Africa — including ZAMRA in Zambia, NAFDAC in Nigeria, and SAHPRA in South Africa — currently operate in a regulatory environment structured around recognition and adoption of decisions made by the US FDA, the European Medicines Agency, and the UK MHRA. An approved drug in a HIC jurisdiction is treated as presumptively approvable in most African jurisdictions; the regulatory evidence base generated in African populations is rarely the primary basis for any approval. This means that treatments for diseases predominantly affecting African populations are approved based on regulatory standards, comparators, and population benchmarks developed elsewhere.

The African Medicines Agency (AMA), whose founding treaty was adopted in 2019, represents the most significant decolonisation initiative in African regulatory science. By creating a continental regulatory harmonisation body with the authority to generate African-specific regulatory decisions — including review of evidence from African trial populations without requiring prior Northern approval — the AMA would alter the direction of regulatory evidence flow. Hoffman and colleagues addressed the obligations of high-income countries in supporting regulatory capacity in low-income settings, arguing that genuine partnership requires transfer of regulatory competence, not merely adoption of Northern regulatory outputs [9].

The role of traditional knowledge in this regulatory picture is significant. Many African medicinal plant treatments have been used for centuries, carry documented community knowledge of efficacy and safety, and exist in a regulatory and IP void that makes them simultaneously unprotectable by African practitioners and appropriable by Northern pharmaceutical companies [10]. Traditional knowledge documentation, combined with African IP protection frameworks and benefit-sharing provisions, is a regulatory decolonisation measure as important as the AMA itself. The pan-African clinical trial registry (PACTR) — an initiative to maintain African trial registration on African institutional infrastructure — similarly represents regulatory and informational sovereignty applied to the trial registration ecosystem itself [6].

## 5. Conclusion

The 24,771 African-site clinical trials registered in AACT represent an enormous accumulation of research effort, participant contribution, and potential knowledge. The structural data — 80% non-African academic sponsorship, geographic concentration in two countries, first authorship below 30%, data held in Northern institutions, publications in Northern journals — demonstrate that this accumulation has not yet produced a decolonised African science. The knowledge flows North; the benefit stays North; the epidemiological questions are set in the North.

Research decolonisation is not an obstacle to rigorous science. It is the condition for more rigorous, more generalisable, and more ethically defensible science. Trials designed around African priority questions, led by African investigators, with data held in African institutions and results published under open-access terms accessible to African clinicians, would produce a body of evidence qualitatively different from — and, for African populations, immeasurably more useful than — the existing extractive model. AU Agenda 2063's commitment to science and technology self-reliance provides the political framework. What is required now is that the 24,771 trials become the foundation of a transformation rather than the continuation of an extraction.
